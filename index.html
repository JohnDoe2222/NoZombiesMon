01

The bottom line

  1. Book one travel-health appointment per family this week. Bring every family member's vaccine records. Children under 5 need a family doctor or travel clinic — Ontario pharmacists only vaccinate age 5 and up, and hepatitis and typhoid shots need a prescription.
  2. Hepatitis A for everyone over age 1. One dose at least two weeks before departure protects for the trip. Children who haven't had their Grade 7 school shots have no hepatitis B protection either — the combined Twinrix Junior shot covers both, with two of three doses done before we fly.
  3. Mosquito bites are the main disease risk, and there's no pill or routine vaccine for it. Dengue, chikungunya and Zika are all present in Jamaica, and regional health authorities flagged rising Caribbean dengue and chikungunya activity this month. Icaridin 20% on everyone over 6 months, every day.
  4. Buy travel medical insurance with evacuation, for every person. OHIP caps emergency hospital care abroad at $50–$400 a day and pays nothing to get you home.
  5. The things most likely to actually hurt a kid on this trip are water, sun and roads — not tropical disease. Assign a water-watcher, pack a travel booster seat, and cover up.

03

Vaccines by age

Pick the group that matches each person. The split that matters is whether they've had Ontario's Grade 7 school shots, which include hepatitis B.

Hepatitis A + B — Twinrix Junior

Recommended

Hepatitis A is the core travel vaccine for Jamaica. Children this age haven't had hepatitis B yet (Ontario gives it free in Grade 7). Twinrix Junior covers both in three doses at 0, 1 and 6 months — two before the trip, the third in spring.

Alternative: a single hepatitis A shot now, and let the free Grade 7 program handle hepatitis B later. Fewer needles; hepatitis B exposure on a resort holiday is uncommon. If you do give Twinrix, tell public health at Grade 7 so the series isn't repeated.

04

Mosquitoes

Dengue, chikungunya and Zika are all established in Jamaica and spread by the same daytime-biting mosquito (Aedes). It bites most in the early morning and late afternoon — beach and pool hours, not just evenings. Transmission peaks in the June–November wet season; late December is the tail end, which lowers the risk but doesn't remove it.

How to apply

  1. Sunscreen first, wait 20 minutes, then repellent. Never use combination sunscreen-repellent products — sunscreen needs reapplying far more often.
  2. Adults apply it to kids. Spray into your hands and spread it on. Children under 10 shouldn't apply their own. Keep it off hands, eyes and mouth.
  3. Reapply after swimming and as the label directs. Wash it off at bedtime.
  4. Cover up at dawn and dusk. Light, loose long sleeves. Permethrin-treated clothing (sold pre-treated in Canada) adds a second layer. Keep room doors and screens shut; air conditioning helps.
Fever rule — read this one twice

If anyone gets a fever in Jamaica or in the 3 weeks after, use acetaminophen (Tylenol) only — no ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) or aspirin until a doctor has ruled out dengue. Those drugs raise the bleeding risk in dengue. Jamaica's health ministry gives the same instruction.

Go to a hospital for severe belly pain, repeated vomiting, bleeding gums or nose, unusual sleepiness or restlessness — especially on days 3–7 as the fever comes down, which is when severe dengue tends to show.

05

Food & water

Traveller's diarrhea is the most likely illness of the trip. Prevention is hand-washing and sealed bottled water; the plan for when it happens is fluids first.

Every day

  • Wash hands before eating; sanitizer when there's no sink.
  • Sealed bottled water, including for brushing teeth with young kids.
  • Hot, freshly cooked food; fruit you peel yourself.

When it happens

  • Oral rehydration salts first — the priority for kids.
  • Loperamide (Imodium): adults and teens only, and never with fever or blood in the stool.
  • Azithromycin (prescription, dosed by weight for kids) only for severe cases: fever, bloody stool, or unable to function.
Get a child seen if

They're peeing much less than usual, can't keep fluids down, are unusually drowsy, have blood in the stool, or a high fever.

Two Jamaica-specific food rules

Skip big reef fish

Barracuda, large grouper and snapper, amberjack and moray eel can carry ciguatera toxin. Cooking and freezing don't destroy it, and you can't taste it. Tell-tale sign: hot things feel cold and cold things feel hot. Never eat fish heads, roe or organs.

Ackee only from a kitchen

Jamaica's national fruit is poisonous unripe and can cause dangerously low blood sugar — children are most at risk. Restaurant and resort ackee is fine. Never let kids eat fruit picked off a tree.

Fresh water and mud

Hurricane Melissa (October 2025) triggered a leptospirosis outbreak in Jamaica — a bacterial infection picked up from water or soil contaminated by animal urine. Stay out of rivers, ponds and standing floodwater, and keep cuts covered on any river or waterfall excursion. Check travel.gc.ca for the current situation before departure.

06

Beach, sun & roads

These are the risks most likely to end a family holiday badly. None needs a needle.

Water

  • One named adult on "water watch" at all times, phone away. Hand off out loud.
  • Many resort pools have no lifeguard.
  • Caught in a current: swim parallel to shore, don't fight it.
  • Life jackets for kids on every boat and excursion.

Sun

  • SPF 30+ broad-spectrum, reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming.
  • Mineral sunscreen (zinc oxide or titanium dioxide) is easier on the reef.
  • Rash guards and wide-brim hats beat sunscreen for kids.
  • Shade 11 am – 3 pm; push fluids.

Roads

  • Taxis and shuttles rarely have car seats — pack a travel booster for any child under 8, 36 kg or 145 cm.
  • Seatbelts on, every ride. Jamaica drives on the left.
  • Avoid road travel after dark where possible.

Feet

  • Water shoes for rocky entries — sea urchins and lionfish spines cause most beach injuries.
  • Look, don't touch: jellyfish, coral, lionfish.

Stings and spines — first aid

  1. Get out of the water. Pain and panic cause drownings.
  2. Jellyfish: rinse with vinegar for at least 30 seconds. No vinegar? Rinse with seawater. Never fresh water, urine or alcohol — they make the stinging cells fire.
  3. Lift off tentacles with tweezers or the edge of a card. Don't rub with a towel or sand.
  4. Hot water soak, 20–45 minutes — as hot as tolerable without scalding (about 42–45°C). Test it on an adult's hand first. The same soak works for sea-urchin and lionfish punctures.
  5. Get help for trouble breathing, chest pain, a sting on the face or over a large area, a spine in a joint, or a child who seems unwell. Ambulance: 110.

09

After we're home

Fever in the first 3 weeks

See a doctor and tell them you were in Jamaica. Dengue can take up to two weeks to show. Acetaminophen only until it's ruled out.

Finish the series

Twinrix dose 3 around May–June 2027. Hepatitis A booster 6–12 months after the first shot. Submit vaccine and prescription receipts to workplace benefits.

Zika — Public Health Agency of Canada guidance
  • Women: wait 2 months after return before trying to conceive.
  • Men: wait 3 months before trying to conceive, and use condoms with any partner for 3 months after return.
  • Partner pregnant at home: condoms, or no sex, for the rest of the pregnancy.
  • Currently pregnant: PHAC no longer advises against travel to Zika-risk areas generally, but does advise avoiding areas with a current outbreak. Talk to your doctor before departure.
